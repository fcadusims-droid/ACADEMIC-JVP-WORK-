# Conversion and Its Counterfeits: Grace, Manipulation, and the Limits of Attitudinal Criteria

João Vitor Perazzolo

28 September 2026

---

## Abstract

Christian traditions that disagree about much else in the doctrine of grace agree on two claims: grace can reorder a person's fundamental loves, and it does so without coercion. The first claim is also true of successful manipulation. This paper asks what the second claim requires. It argues for a structural constraint. A manipulated change can in principle share the whole attitudinal profile of a conversion: the values acquired, their endorsement, and the agent's continuing capacities. So no criterion of grace that consists of attitudinal features can tell grace from its counterfeits. Preparation, fruits and perseverance can serve as defeasible signs of grace, never as its criteria. The constraint reconstructs two old judgments as one: the rejection of semi-Pelagianism and the refusal to certify grace by its affections. Identifying grace by its divine source does not escape the constraint, because a source condition fixes the presence of a divine cause but not its manner, and manipulation arguments in the free-will literature already use a divine designer. What remains is a condition on manner. The traditions' own descriptions converge on one: grace heals the capacity by which a person perceives and answers to value, rather than installing values. The paper states this condition in interventionist terms, shows what it can and cannot exclude, and draws its consequence for assurance: certainty of grace cannot rest on introspection of one's own conversion.

**Keywords:** grace; conversion; manipulation; assurance; operating and cooperating grace; theological compatibilism

---

## 1. Introduction

Western Christian traditions that anathematize one another's doctrines of justification agree that grace does not coerce. "He who made you without you," preached Augustine, whom all of them claim, "does not justify you without you" (*Sermo* 169.11.13). The Council of Trent condemns the view that the will moved by God does nothing, "as something inanimate" (Session VI, can. 4). The Synod of Dort denies that grace treats human beings "as senseless stocks and blocks" (III/IV, art. 16). The Formula of Concord denies that the Spirit's impulse is "a *coactio*, or coercion" (Solid Declaration II.64). These traditions disagree about how much the human will contributes to its own conversion. They agree that whatever grace does, it does not do what a coercer or a manipulator does.

That agreement carries a cost that recent philosophy has made easier to see. Work on personal autonomy has shown that manipulation can in principle produce an agent whose values, endorsements and capacities match those of an agent who acquired them in the ordinary way (Mele 1995; Pereboom 2001). One of the best known cases has a goddess as the manipulator (Mele 2006, 188–89). If the attitudinal profile of a conversion can be reproduced by manipulation, then whatever distinguishes grace from its counterfeits is not in the profile. A theology that affirms both that grace transforms and that it does not manipulate owes an account of where the difference lies.

This paper argues five claims:

1. A structural constraint: no criterion of grace that is a function of the attitudinal profile alone can tell grace from every counterfeit (§3).
2. Three familiar attitudinal marks of grace, namely preparation, fruits and perseverance, can therefore be signs of grace but not criteria of it. The rejection of semi-Pelagianism and the refusal to certify grace by its affections are two applications of the same constraint (§4).
3. Identifying grace by its divine source does not remove the problem. A source condition fixes the presence of a divine cause but not the manner of its action, and manipulation arguments have been applied to a divine designer (§5).
4. What remains is a condition on manner. The traditions' own descriptions of that manner converge on healing rather than installing: grace restores the capacity by which a person perceives and answers to value, and does not set the values directly. §6 states this condition in interventionist terms, argues that it meets requirements the tradition imposes, and says what it leaves unresolved.
5. Certainty of grace cannot be grounded in introspection of one's own conversion. The main doctrines of assurance differ in where they place certainty, and the constraint predicts which placements are available (§7).

The paper is a work of analytic theology (Crisp and Rea 2009). Its method is a grammar of consistency: it shows what certain claims about grace commit one to, and which combinations of claims fail. It does not argue for any doctrine of grace. It does not derive doctrine from formal results. It does not decide between monergist and synergist accounts or between Thomist and Molinist accounts of efficacious grace. The attitudinal criteria in §4 are types, not traditions. The traditions discussed have used these types as signs, and they have resources to avoid using them as criteria. The formal element is modest: one valid argument in §3 and one causal statement of a condition in §6. Each is there to do a specific job, stated where it is used.

The constraint is adapted from a companion paper, which argues that formal models directing or evaluating value change by the agent's attitudes cannot distinguish conversion from manipulation (Perazzolo 2026). Section 3 restates the argument in a self-contained form, so that nothing here depends on that paper.

## 2. What the Traditions Agree On

### 2.1 Operating and cooperating grace

The distinction most widely shared in Western accounts of grace and freedom is Augustine's, as Aquinas received it: "He operates that we may will; and when we will, He cooperates that we may perfect" (Augustine, *De gratia et libero arbitrio* 17.33, quoted in Aquinas, *ST* I-II q. 111 a. 2).[^1] Aquinas glosses the distinction in terms of the agent's role in a given effect. Grace is operating "in that effect in which our mind is moved and does not move, but in which God is the sole mover"; it is cooperating "in that effect in which our mind both moves and is moved" (*ST* I-II q. 111 a. 2). The distinction does not say that operating grace acts on an inert thing. It says that in a certain effect the mind does not contribute a motion of its own.

### 2.2 Non-coercion described as manner

When the traditions say why grace is not coercion, they speak of how grace acts, not of what it produces.

For Aquinas, "God moves everything in its own manner," as heavy and light things are moved differently according to their natures. God "so infuses the gift of justifying grace that at the same time He moves the free-will to accept the gift of grace, in such as are capable of being moved thus" (*ST* I-II q. 113 a. 3). The principle stated at the opening of the *Summa*, that "grace does not destroy nature but perfects it" (*ST* I q. 1 a. 8 ad 2), is here applied to the will.

Trent anathematizes the view that the free will "moved and excited by God ... cannot refuse its consent, if it would, but that, as something inanimate, it does nothing whatever and is merely passive" (Session VI, can. 4).

The Canons of Dort head their Third and Fourth Main Points "Of the Corruption of Man, His Conversion to God, and the Manner Thereof." They teach that regeneration is not accomplished "merely by the external preaching of the gospel, by moral suasion," and that "the will thus renewed is not only actuated and influenced by God, but in consequence of this influence, becomes itself active" (III/IV, art. 12). They deny that grace treats human beings "as senseless stocks and blocks, nor takes away their will and its properties." Instead, grace "spiritually quickens, heals, corrects, and at the same time sweetly and powerfully bends it," so that "the true and spiritual restoration and freedom of our will" consists in the obedience that follows (III/IV, art. 16).

The Formula of Concord holds that "God does not force man to become godly," but draws him "in such a way that his darkened understanding is turned into an enlightened one and his perverse will into an obedient one." The impulse of the Spirit "is not a *coactio*, or coercion" (Solid Declaration II.60, II.64).

Eastern Orthodox theology describes the relation as a co-working of two wills. For Lossky, deification requires "the concurrence of two wills": "the divine and deifying will granting grace," and "the human will which submits to the will of God in receiving grace and making it its own" (1976, 126–27).

### 2.3 Where the traditions disagree

The traditions disagree sharply about the agent's contribution at the first moment of conversion.

- Trent requires that the will moved by God "co-operates towards disposing and preparing itself" (Session VI, can. 4).
- The Westminster Confession holds that in effectual calling the person is "altogether passive therein, until, being quickened and renewed by the Holy Spirit, he is thereby enabled to answer this call" (10.2).
- The Formula of Concord calls conversion "not only in part, but altogether an operation, gift, and present, and work of the Holy Ghost alone" (Solid Declaration II.89). It adds that once regeneration has begun, "through the power of the Holy Ghost we can and should cooperate, although still in great weakness" (II.65).

These are disagreements about whether the agent contributes a motion of her own at a given point. They are not disagreements about whether grace works on the will *as a will*. On that question, traditions that condemn one another's accounts of passivity use one vocabulary, and it is a vocabulary of manner:

- "in its own manner";
- "not as something inanimate";
- "not as stocks and blocks";
- "sweetly";
- "heals".

None of these texts claims that grace differs from coercion by its effects. None says, beyond these images, what the manner consists in. The rest of this paper works in that gap.

## 3. The Constraint

### 3.1 Attitudinal profiles

Let the *attitudinal profile* of a change in a person's values between two times be the sequence of her values over the interval, together with every attitude she holds toward them. This includes:

- how her earlier values rank the prospect of the later ones;
- how her later values rank the memory of the earlier;
- whether, reflecting on the change, she endorses it;
- her standing capacities at each time: whether she deliberates, responds to reasons and answers for her past acts.

The profile excludes facts about what caused the change, except as those facts are themselves attitudes or capacities of the agent.

A conversion, in the sense relevant here, has four marks:

- **(F) Fundamental.** It alters what the person values non-derivatively, not only her beliefs about how to get what she already values.
- **(U) Unanchored.** Her prior values do not rank the change as an improvement.
- **(E) Endorsed.** Her later values rank it as an improvement, and she endorses it on reflection.
- **(A) Agency preserved.** She remains an agent who deliberates, responds to reasons and answers for what she did before.

Mark (U) is what distinguishes conversion from refinement, the development of a value one already grasps, however defectively. It is also what theological accounts of conversion characteristically require. Lonergan describes conversion as an "about-face" into a horizon partly at odds with the old one (1972, 237–38). A horizon that is at odds with the old one cannot be underwritten by it. The Council of Orange puts the point in the register of grace. Canon 5 condemns anyone who says that "not only the increase of faith but also its beginning and the very desire for faith ... belongs to us by nature and not by a gift of grace."

### 3.2 Counterfeits

The four marks are properties of the profile, and a manipulated change can have all four. In Mele's (1995) case, an agent's values are engineered to match those of another person who acquired them in the ordinary way, including whatever sense of their rightness that person has. In the zygote case, the goddess Diana creates a zygote in a deterministic world so that, thirty years later, the man it becomes, Ernie, performs a particular act. By stipulation, Ernie is normal in every respect a compatibilist would check (Mele 2006, 188–89). In Pereboom's four-case argument, an agent's reasoning is manipulated, first moment by moment and then at progressively greater remove, while it remains reasons-responsive and his own by every structural test (2001, 112–15).

In each case what makes the change manipulation is not a feature of the resulting psychology but the history that produced it. The contested sociology of new religious movements has the same structure. Whether converts were persuaded or brainwashed was a live question because the two were taken to differ while producing similar members (Barker 1984). The field's division between "passive" and "active" paradigms of the convert is a dispute about the process, not about the outcome (Richardson 1985).

### 3.3 The argument

1. **(Possibility)** For any conversion, a counterfeit with the same attitudinal profile is possible.
2. **(Adequacy)** Grace and its counterfeits differ.
3. **Therefore**, no criterion that is a function of the attitudinal profile alone distinguishes grace from every counterfeit.

The inference is valid, and trivially so: a function of the profile cannot separate cases that the profile does not separate. The content lies in the premises. Adequacy is granted by every tradition in §2; §8.1 considers a way of weakening it. Possibility is the premise a theologian is most likely to contest, and §5.4 examines the most important attempt to do so.

Two distinctions sharpen the conclusion.

**Metaphysical and epistemic possibility.** Possibility can be read metaphysically: there could be a counterfeit with the same profile. It can also be read epistemically: for all the agent can tell from within, her own case might be one. A theology can deny the first and grant the second. The second is enough for the argument of §7.

**Criteria and signs.** A *criterion* of grace is a feature whose presence settles that a change was grace. A *sign* is a feature that is evidence for it, defeasibly. The constraint rules out profile-based criteria. It says nothing against profile-based signs. A sign can remain good evidence even if counterfeits are possible, provided they are rare.

### 3.4 Presence and manner

Conditions that look beyond the profile to the history of the change are of two kinds, and only one of them helps.

A *presence* condition asks whether an external influence was causally involved in the change. Research on artificial agents that shape their users' preferences has proposed such a test. It judges a change in a person's preferences against the change they would have undergone without the system's influence (Carroll et al. 2022). A presence test catches manipulation. It also catches every change that an outside influence occasions. Theology has a specific reason to reject it: on every account in §2, grace ordinarily comes through means, such as preaching, sacrament and the witness of others. A presence criterion would make every conversion under preaching suspect.

A *manner* condition asks how the influence acted on the agent: whether it engaged her capacities or bypassed them (Mele 1995; Christman 1991; Fischer and Ravizza 1998).

There is one other way to escape the constraint. It is to evaluate the change by an attitude-independent standard, asking whether the new values are in fact better or whether the convert grasps a reason that is really there. That route cannot be sufficient for theology. A manipulator who installs true values by bypassing the will performs a *benevolent* manipulation. Its outcome is good, and it is still what Trent's canon 4 and Dort's article 16 deny that grace does. The traditions of §2 are therefore committed to a manner condition, whatever else they add.

## 4. Three Attitudinal Criteria

An attitudinal criterion of grace takes its standard from some part of the profile:

- the attitudes before the change;
- the attitudes after it;
- a rule applied to the whole course of the person's life.

Each has a familiar theological form.

### 4.1 Preparation

On an ex ante criterion, grace is present where a prior disposition, such as a longing for God, fear of judgement or a readiness to believe, leads into the change. The criterion faces a dilemma.

Suppose first that the preparation is a natural disposition that recommends the change. Then the change is anchored in what the agent already valued. It is a refinement and not a conversion, and grace becomes the completion of a trajectory the agent was on. This is the view Orange's canon 5 condemns when it assigns to grace not only the increase of faith but "its beginning and the very desire for faith."

Suppose instead that the preparation is itself an effect of grace. Trent takes this horn: prevenient grace precedes any disposing, and the person "is not able, by his own free will, without the grace of God, to move himself unto justice" (Session VI, ch. 5). Then the preparation cannot be the criterion of grace, because whether the preparation was grace is the same question over again. Adding further profile features does not stop the regress, since each is open to the same doubt.

There is a further limit. By mark (U), the agent's prior values rank her conversion as no improvement. They rank corruption and unanchored manipulation the same way. The ex ante standpoint cannot tell the three apart. It registers only that each carries the agent away from what she valued.

Read this way, the rejection of semi-Pelagianism and the constraint agree at the first step of conversion. A preparation criterion either makes conversion a refinement or presupposes the distinction it was meant to supply.

### 4.2 Fruits

On an ex post criterion, grace is present where the change is endorsed and bears fruit: love, joy, peace and holiness of life. The criterion fails by self-ratification. A process that installs values can install their endorsement, and the fruits of a value are expressions of it in affection and conduct. They belong to the profile.

The tradition knew this. Much of Jonathan Edwards's *Religious Affections* (1746) is organized around it. Its second part lists features that are "no certain signs" that affections are gracious, among them:

- that the affections are raised very high;
- that they have great effects on the body;
- that they make persons fluent and fervent in talk about religion;
- that they come with texts of Scripture remarkably brought to mind;
- that they show an appearance of love;
- that they make persons zealous in external duties of worship;
- that they make persons confident of their own good estate.

Read with the constraint in view, the list is a catalogue of profile features that a counterfeit can share.

Aquinas reaches the same verdict by a different route. One may know that one has grace "conjecturally by signs," for instance "when he is conscious of delighting in God, and of despising worldly things." But no one can know it with certainty by himself, "for certitude about a thing can only be had when we may judge of it by its proper principle," and the principle of grace, God, exceeds our knowledge (*ST* I-II q. 112 a. 5). His reason is not the present one. His verdict is the same: fruits yield conjecture.

The convert's own ex post view has the same limit. Because conversion is transformative, the convert finds it hard to assess whether she is now better placed to judge than she was (De Cruz 2018).

Edwards's twelfth positive sign is that gracious affections "have their exercise and fruit in Christian practice." He calls it "the chief of all the signs of grace, both as an evidence of the sincerity of professors unto others, and also to their own consciences." It is also a profile feature. A sustained counterfeit yields sustained practice, and the disputes over new religious movements presuppose exactly that (Barker 1984). Practice is the best sign, not a criterion.

### 4.3 Perseverance

A third criterion takes no single time as authoritative. It applies a rule to the whole course of a life: a true conversion is one that perseveres to the end. Two objections apply.

First, perseverance is still a feature of the profile, extended in time. A counterfeit whose effects last also perseveres.

Second, the criterion is unavailable to anyone before the end. Augustine devotes a chapter of *De correptione et gratia* to the point; its English translators head it "No one is certain and secure of his own predestination and salvation" (13.40). Trent condemns anyone who claims, without special revelation, absolute certainty that he will persevere (Session VI, can. 16).

Perseverance is thus a retrospective sign. It cannot be introspected in advance, and it cannot be a criterion even afterwards.

### 4.4 What the three show

None of this shows that the traditions are incoherent. A theology would be inconsistent only if it held three things at once:

1. that one of these attitudinal features *is* the criterion of grace;
2. that grace differs from its counterfeits (Adequacy);
3. that counterfeits with the same profile are possible (Possibility).

The main traditions do not hold (1). They treat preparation, fruits and perseverance as signs. What the constraint adds is an explanation of why those signs could never be promoted to criteria, however refined, and a single account of two judgments usually made separately:

- **Against semi-Pelagianism.** The beginning of conversion cannot be certified by what the agent brought to it.
- **Against enthusiasm.** Its genuineness cannot be certified by what it produced.

The constraint also bears on practice. Any procedure that admits persons to a community, or assures them of their state, by testing a narrative or an affection against a template treats a sign as a criterion. The constraint says what such a procedure can deliver: evidence, not verdicts.

## 5. Source Conditions and the Problem of Divine Manipulation

### 5.1 Decree as a source condition

A different kind of account identifies grace not by any attitude but by its source: grace is the change God works in those God has chosen. A decree or election is not a feature of the attitudinal profile, so a source condition escapes the constraint in form. The question is whether it supplies what Adequacy requires.

### 5.2 Presence of a divine cause is not enough

A source condition says that God was causally involved. That is a presence condition (§3.4) with a divine agent.

If "caused by God" were sufficient for grace, then a change produced by God in the way Diana produces Ernie's act would count as grace. The difference between grace and manipulation would then be a difference of who acts, not how. That is not what the traditions of §2 say. They do not claim merely that God is not a manipulator. They claim that grace does not work as a manipulator works: not on stocks and blocks, not on something inanimate.

The free-will literature has drawn the consequence explicitly. Manipulation arguments have been applied to divine determinism at length (Furlong 2019, ch. 3). Divine election and hardening have been argued to make God resemble a covert manipulator, posing "significant challenges" for Christian divine determinists (Visala 2023). One compatibilist reply to manipulation arguments locates the difference in causal sourcehood: the source of a manipulated agent's action "lies outside of the agent in the intentions of the manipulator" (Deery and Nahmias 2017, 1255). Applied to a God who intends a particular conversion, that criterion appears to put divine determination on the side of manipulation rather than rescue it. A source condition therefore needs a manner condition as much as any other account does.

### 5.3 The non-competition reply

A strong theological reply denies that divine causation belongs on the same plane as a manipulator's. Tanner states two rules for talk of God and creation (1988, 47). The first is to avoid both simple univocity and simple contrast between divine and created predicates. The second is to avoid "in talk about God's creative agency all suggestions of limitation in scope or manner." From these she concludes that divine and created agency do not compete: a non-contrastively transcendent God can be "the immediate source of being of every sort" (46), including the creature's own acting. Grant (2019) develops an account on which God directly causes every free act without any prior divine state sufficient for it. Couenhoven (2013) defends an Augustinian compatibilism on which responsibility does not require libertarian freedom.

These accounts answer one question: whether God's causing a willing makes it not the creature's own. They do not answer another: what the effect of divine causation in the creature consists in. A non-competitive cause could produce in the creature either a restored capacity or an installed value. Non-competition may be what a theological compatibilist needs against the manipulation argument, but it does not supply the manner the traditions describe.

### 5.4 Edwards: denying Possibility

Edwards takes the other route. His first positive sign is itself a source condition: "Affections that are truly spiritual and gracious, do arise from those influences and operations on the heart, which are spiritual, supernatural and divine." He adds a claim about the profile. In gracious affections there is "a new inward perception or sensation of their minds, entirely different in its nature and kind, from anything that ever their minds were the subjects of before they were sanctified." This is "not a new faculty of understanding, but it is a new foundation laid in the nature of the soul, for a new kind of exercises." The devil can suggest thoughts and excite ideas in the imagination; of such impressions Edwards says "there is nothing in their nature above the power of the devil." He denies that the devil can produce the new sense (Edwards 1746, pt. III, sign 1).

This is a denial of Possibility for creaturely counterfeiters. On Edwards's view, the profile of true grace contains an element no creature can install. Two limits follow.

First, the denial concerns creaturely counterfeiters. It does not address a divine one, which is the case the manipulation argument of §5.2 concerns.

Second, and more important, the denial is metaphysical, not epistemic. The whole second part of *Religious Affections* exists because persons who lack the new sense take its counterfeits for it. If the new sense were unmistakable from within, the list of no-signs would be pastorally idle. Edwards thus illustrates the distinction drawn in §3.3. A theology can hold that grace and its counterfeits differ in the profile while granting that the difference cannot be reliably discerned from inside the profile. Section 7 turns on this.

## 6. The Manner of Grace

### 6.1 Four requirements

The traditions of §2 impose four requirements on any manner condition:

- **(R1) No natural beginning.** Grace must be able to produce a change that the agent's prior values do not endorse, and the beginning of the change must not be something the agent contributes on her own (Orange, can. 5; §4.1).
- **(R2) No bypassing.** Grace must not act on the will as a manipulator does (Trent, Session VI, can. 4; Dort III/IV, art. 16; Formula of Concord, Solid Declaration II.64).
- **(R3) Not a presence condition.** External influence as such must not count against a change, since grace comes through means (§3.4).
- **(R4) Benevolent manipulation excluded.** Installing true values by bypassing the will must not count as grace (§3.4).

R1 and R2 pull against each other. The natural way to prevent bypassing is to give the agent a controlling role, such as consent or non-resistance. A controlling role grounded in the agent's prior attitudes either anchors the change or makes its beginning her own, and so violates R1. Removing the control preserves R1 but raises again the question of what keeps grace from bypassing. This is the problem of grace and freedom in the form the constraint gives it.

### 6.2 Existing candidates

Three candidates in the literature can be read as manner conditions.

**Response to second-order volitions (Stump 1988).** Stump argued that God may alter a person's first-order will without destroying his freedom when he has a second-order volition for the change. Her example is Patricius, whose prayer "expresses a second-order volition for God to alter Patricius's first-order will" (1988, 412). "Where the requisite second-order desires and volitions are absent, God cannot alter the first-order desires without undermining or destroying freedom of will" (414). This meets R2 on a structural account of freedom. It violates R1: the change is anchored in a volition the agent already has. It models sanctification, the growth of a will already turned toward God, and not the conversion that turns it.

**Quiescence.** Stump later proposed that the will can cease resisting grace without willing the good; in that quiescent state grace acts (Stump 2001; 2003, ch. 13). The proposal aims at R1, since quiescence is not a desire for the good and so does not anchor the change in the good it turns to. It aims at R2 as well, since the agent's non-resistance gates grace. Timpe (2007) argues that the agent can then control whether grace takes effect without causing it. Kittle (2015) argues that Timpe's version does not escape semi-Pelagianism and that two alternative models both fall short, one less badly than the other; see also DeWeese-Boyd (2006). In the terms of §6.1 this debate is the R1–R2 tension. If the agent controls the gate, the beginning of the change is hers, however thin, and that is the semi-Pelagian worry. If she does not, the question of bypassing returns.

**Sufficient grace.** The Molinist account holds that grace makes a right response possible without determining it. It was one side of the dispute *de auxiliis*, which Paul V closed in 1607 by allowing each party "to defend its own doctrine" and enjoining each "from censuring or condemning the opposite opinion" (Astrain 1908). The account addresses whether grace determines the response. It does not address what grace acts on.

All three candidates specify the agent's gating role: whether and when grace acts, and whether it determines the outcome. None specifies the *object* of grace's action. The constraint suggests that this is the missing variable.

### 6.3 Proposal: the target of grace

The following is a proposal, offered for assessment rather than as a result.

**The model.** Represent a person's evaluative life by five variables:

- $C$, her evaluative capacity: her ability to perceive and respond to reasons of value;
- $R$, the reasons and considerations available to her;
- $X$, her evaluative activity: attending, weighing and responding;
- $V$, her values;
- $E$, her endorsements.

In the ordinary course, $X$ depends on $C$ and $R$, $V$ on $X$, and $E$ on $V$ and $X$.

**Two kinds of influence.** An influence is *value-installing* if its target is $V$ or $E$, or $X$ steered toward a fixed outcome. It sets the values or endorsements directly, written $do(V = v_1)$, or it drives the agent's activity to a predetermined result. Either way, the agent's own responsiveness to reasons is left idle or recruited only to ratify. An influence is *capacity-restoring* if its only target is $C$. It sets the capacity toward its proper functioning, $do(C = c^*)$, and the new values arise from the agent's own activity, $V_1 = g(X(c^*, R))$.[^2]

Three observations follow.

1. The two kinds of influence can yield the same profile. An installer who also leaves the agent's capacities intact, or sets them, produces an agent whose standing capacities match those of a restored agent. The constraint of §3 applies to them.
2. A presence test does not separate them. Under either influence the agent's values would have been different without it.
3. The target separates them, and it is a fact about the history of the change, not about its profile.

**The requirements.** The condition meets the four requirements of §6.1, with one qualification for R2 that §6.4 takes up:

- **R1.** Restoring a capacity does not require the endorsement of the agent's prior values; a person whose capacity is damaged cannot evaluate the goods it fails to perceive. And the change begins with the restoration, not with an act the agent contributes on her own.
- **R2.** A restoring influence does not bypass the agent. Its object is the capacity whose exercise produces the new values. The exception is an influence that sets the capacity in order to produce a chosen verdict ("Aim," §6.4).
- **R3.** The condition is silent about the source of the influence. A teacher, a friend or a text can restore a capacity, so grace through means passes.
- **R4.** A benevolent manipulator sets the values, however good they are. Setting values is installation.

**Fit with the texts.** The condition fits the traditions' own vocabulary closely:

- Dort says that grace "quickens, heals, corrects" the will and that "the true and spiritual restoration and freedom of our will" consists in the obedience that follows (III/IV, art. 16).
- Aquinas describes habitual grace as "a habitual gift whereby corrupted human nature is healed, and after being healed is lifted up" (*ST* I-II q. 109 a. 9). He says that God moves "everything in its own manner" (q. 113 a. 3).
- Edwards describes the new sense as "not a new faculty ... but ... a new foundation laid in the nature of the soul."
- Lossky says the human will "acts by grace to the extent in which nature participates in grace, in which the likeness is restored" (1976, 127).

**Operating and cooperating grace.** On this reading, operating grace is the restoration, $do(C = c^*)$, and cooperating grace is the agent's activity under the restored capacity. This also reconciles two formulas that look opposed. Trent denies that the will is passive "as something inanimate." Westminster holds that the person is "altogether passive" until "quickened and renewed" and so "enabled to answer." In restoration the agent is passive as a patient is passive in being healed, not as a stone is passive. Once restored, the will "becomes itself active" (Dort III/IV, art. 12). The two formulas may still disagree about when the agent's activity begins. They need not disagree about what grace acts on.

**Relation to Deery and Nahmias.** The proposal is related to Deery and Nahmias's (2017) interventionist treatment of manipulation, but it asks a different question. They ask where the causal source of an action lies. The target condition asks which variable in the agent's evaluative life an influence sets.

### 6.4 What the condition does not settle

**Proper function.** "Restoration" presupposes a norm for the capacity: what it is for it to function well. Here an attitude-independent standard enters, as §3.4 said one might. A theological anthropology supplies the norm, in which human nature is ordered to God. The condition is therefore not neutral between theological and secular accounts of value. That is acceptable in a theological paper, but it means the condition will not persuade a reader who rejects such a norm. Whether an influence restored or installed also depends on facts about proper function, not on the agent's attitudes. That is consistent with the constraint, and it is also why §7 follows.

**Aim.** The target condition classifies Mele's engineered agent and Pereboom's first case as installation: the first sets the agent's values, and the second steers his deliberation moment by moment. The zygote case is harder. Diana acts upstream: she sets initial conditions, not values, and Ernie's values arise from his own development. What makes the case troubling is Diana's aim. She chose the initial conditions so as to produce a particular act, because she wanted that act, not because it was what the reasons supported. The target condition must therefore be supplemented by a condition on aim. Restoration aims at the capacity's proper function, and so at whatever verdict the reasons support. Installation aims at a particular verdict, whatever the reasons support. A good teacher aims at a verdict too, but only as what a working capacity will find, and would count the teaching successful if the reasons turned out to support a different one. For God, whose knowledge of what the reasons support is complete, aiming at function and aiming at the verdict may coincide. Whether they can be distinguished is, in substance, what the parties *de auxiliis* disputed, and incompatibilists deny that any determining aim is compatible with the agent's responsibility (Pereboom 2001; 2014). The condition does not resolve this. It relocates the question from "Does God cause the conversion?" to "Does God's causing aim at the capacity or at the verdict?"

**Distinctiveness.** Capacity restoration is not unique to grace. Good teachers, friendships and therapies restore capacities, and some conversions under them are not religious. The manner condition separates grace from manipulation. It does not identify grace. What the traditions treat as proper to grace is its source and its scope, the restoration of the capacity for God, not a manner found nowhere else.

**Introspection.** Whether an influence targeted the capacity or the values is a fact about the history of the change. A restored capacity and an installed value can present alike from within.

## 7. Assurance

### 7.1 The consequence

Suppose grace is distinguished from its counterfeits by manner, and manner is a fact about the causal history of a change. Then no inspection of one's own attitudinal profile can certify that one's conversion was grace. This is not a claim about how confident believers feel. It is a claim about what can ground certainty: nothing in the profile. The main doctrines of assurance can be read as three different responses to this consequence.

### 7.2 Three responses

**Accepting the limit.** Aquinas allows knowledge of one's grace by special revelation, denies certainty "by himself," and allows conjecture by signs (*ST* I-II q. 112 a. 5). Trent holds that "no one can know with a certainty of faith, which cannot be subject to error, that he has obtained the grace of God" (Session VI, ch. 9; cf. cans. 13–16). This is the constraint accepted as an epistemic limit.

**A factive ground.** The Westminster Confession rejects the conjectural option in terms that read almost as a reply to Aquinas. Assurance "is not a bare conjectural and probable persuasion grounded upon a fallible hope; but an infallible assurance of faith." It is founded on three things:

- "the divine truth of the promises of salvation";
- "the inward evidence of those graces unto which these promises are made";
- "the testimony of the Spirit of adoption witnessing with our spirits that we are the children of God" (18.2).

The constraint sorts these grounds.

- **Inward evidence.** The inward evidence of graces is profile evidence. By the constraint it is conjectural, and it cannot carry the word "infallible."
- **The promises.** The promises are not about the believer's profile at all.
- **The testimony of the Spirit.** If genuine, the testimony is a ground outside the profile. It corresponds to the factive route of §3.4: a real reason actually grasped.

Plantinga's extended Aquinas/Calvin model gives this route an epistemology. Beliefs produced by the inward instigation of the Holy Spirit are warranted when "produced in them by a belief-producing process that is functioning properly in an appropriate cognitive environment ... according to a design plan successfully aimed at the production of true beliefs" (2000, 246). Warrant on this model depends on the actual process, not on what introspection can discriminate.

The constraint and Westminster are consistent, then, if "infallible" is read externally. The assurance is infallible when it is in fact the Spirit's testimony. That is not the claim that the believer can infallibly tell the testimony from a counterfeit by looking within. The Confession's next section fits this reading: assurance "doth not so belong to the essence of faith, but that a true believer may wait long, and conflict with many difficulties before he be partaker of it" (18.3).

**Certainty directed at the object.** Calvin defines faith as "a firm and sure knowledge of the divine favor toward us, founded on the truth of a free promise in Christ, and revealed to our minds, and sealed on our hearts, by the Holy Spirit" (*Institutes* 3.2.7). The certainty is directed at the promise, and the promise is not about the believer's profile. The constraint does not touch certainty about the promise. It touches only the reflexive step, *and I am one who has received it by grace*. Calvin routes that step through the Spirit's sealing, that is, back to the second response.

### 7.3 What follows

The constraint predicts where a doctrine of assurance can place certainty, if it claims certainty at all:

- not in the profile;
- possibly in the object of faith;
- possibly in a factive divine testimony, provided the testimony's certainty is not taken to include the believer's ability to discriminate it from within.

The traditions that deny certainty and those that affirm it both respect this prediction in their most careful statements.

The constraint does not rule out historical evidence. A record of how a change came about, for instance that it came through ordinary means that engaged a person's capacities, is evidence about manner. It is defeasible, but it bears on the right variable. That is arguably what pastoral practice has been doing when it asks for a conversion narrative rather than only a present state, provided the narrative is treated as evidence and not as a test.

## 8. Objections

### 8.1 "God cannot manipulate, by definition"

One might hold that whatever God does to a will is by definition not manipulation. Adequacy then becomes a distinction between grace and creaturely manipulation only, and the argument of §5.2 lapses. The reply is that the texts of §2 do not argue from the identity of the agent. They describe how grace acts: it heals, it does not treat the will as a stone. A definitional reply secures the distinction by stipulation and gives up the content the traditions gave it. Mele's choice of a goddess shows that the structural worry does not depend on the manipulator being a creature.

### 8.2 "This is the old problem of the discernment of spirits"

In content, largely yes. The paper concedes this, and the concession narrows its claim to novelty. What the constraint adds is:

- an explanation of why discernment by effects cannot be decisive;
- the identification of the anti-semi-Pelagian and anti-enthusiast judgments as one judgment;
- a specification of what a condition outside the profile must fix: the target and aim of the influence, not its presence and not only the agent's gating role.

The contribution is a reconstruction and a proposal, not a new doctrine.

### 8.3 "Regeneration creates a new nature; restoration is too weak"

Monergist traditions describe regeneration as a new heart, not a repair. The Canons of Dort, which are as monergist as any confession, describe the work in the language of healing and restoration (III/IV, art. 16), and Edwards calls the new sense "not a new faculty." The target condition requires only that grace act on the capacity rather than on the verdicts. How deep the capacity's renewal goes, whether healing or new creation, it leaves open.

### 8.4 "Why formal apparatus at all?"

The causal statement of §6.3 does one job. It shows that presence and target come apart: a counterfactual test of presence does not separate restoration from installation, and a test of target does. Without that, "manner" risks remaining the metaphor the tradition already had.

## 9. Conclusion

The traditions examined here agree that grace differs from coercion and manipulation by its manner, not by its effects. This paper has argued that the agreement has a structure. The attitudinal profile of a conversion can be shared by a counterfeit, so no profile-based criterion can tell them apart. Preparation, fruits and perseverance are signs, not criteria. The rejection of semi-Pelagianism and the refusal to certify grace by its affections are two applications of one constraint. A source condition is needed but does not settle the question, since a divine cause, like any cause, can in principle act in more than one manner.

What remains is a condition on manner. The proposal of §6 is that grace, as the traditions describe it, targets the capacity by which a person perceives and answers to value, and does not set values directly. Supplemented by a condition on aim, it meets the requirements the traditions impose. It requires a theological norm of proper function. It leaves open whether divine aim at a restored capacity can be distinguished from aim at its verdict, which is the old question *de auxiliis* in a new form. Because it is a condition on history, it implies that certainty of grace cannot be found by introspecting one's own conversion. The main doctrines of assurance, read carefully, locate certainty accordingly: in the object of faith or in a divine testimony, never in the self-inspected profile.
