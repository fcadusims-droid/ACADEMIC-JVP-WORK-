## The claim in one paragraph

Formal models of value change (decision theories for changing selves, preference-change models,
value-learning agents, corrigibility schemes) are increasingly used to *direct* and *evaluate*
value change, not only to describe it. To do that they need a reference standard, and the models
on offer, with one exception, take it from the agent's attitudes: her values before the change,
her values after it, or a fixed higher-order rule over both. The paper argues that none of them
can evaluate *conversion*, an undergone reordering of fundamental values that the agent comes
to endorse, as distinct from *manipulation*, because a manipulator can in principle produce the
same attitudinal profile. A model adequate to conversion needs an attitude-independent standard
or a condition on the history of the change.

## Three standpoints, three failures

- **Ex ante** (the values before the change): registers any unanchored change as no improvement,
  and typically as a loss.
- **Ex post** (the values after it): ratifies whatever change an intervention produces, because
  the endorsement is produced by the change it evaluates.
- **Higher-order** (a fixed rule over both, as in Pettigrew's aggregation or value learning):
  cannot be revised by the change it evaluates, and when its weights track shared values it
  discounts large value change whatever its source.

The three correspond to the cybernetic tradition's three ways of directing a system: feedback,
tracking and optimization. The argument does not depend on that correspondence.

## The models, case by case

Callard (anchored change, conceded), Paul, Pettigrew and Bykvist, Dietrich and List, Hansson,
Bradley, and value learning and corrigibility. The exception is influence-aware alignment
(Carroll et al. 2022), which uses a historical benchmark: it penalizes a change the agent would
not have undergone without the system. That tracks the *source* of the change rather than its
*manner*, so it penalizes a conversion occasioned by a person along with a manipulation.

## What would count against it

The argument is valid, so it fails only through a premise: a formal model that ranks some
conversion above an attitudinally identical manipulation; a mark of conversion that no
capacity-bypassing process could produce; or a reason to think the difference is normatively
idle. The paper states each.
