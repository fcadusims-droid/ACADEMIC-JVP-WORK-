## The claim in one paragraph

Christian traditions that disagree about much in the doctrine of grace agree on two claims:
grace can reorder a person's fundamental loves, and it does so without coercion. The first claim
is also true of successful manipulation. The paper argues that a manipulated change can in
principle share the whole *attitudinal profile* of a conversion (the values acquired, their
endorsement, the agent's continuing capacities), so no criterion of grace that consists of
attitudinal features can tell grace from its counterfeits. What remains is a condition on the
*manner* of the change.

## The argument in five steps

- **The constraint (§3).** For any conversion a counterfeit with the same profile is possible;
  grace and its counterfeits differ; so no function of the profile alone separates them.
- **Signs, not criteria (§4).** Preparation, fruits and perseverance are defeasible signs of
  grace. The rejection of semi-Pelagianism and the refusal to certify grace by its affections
  are read as two applications of the same constraint.
- **Source is not enough (§5).** Identifying grace by its divine source fixes the presence of a
  divine cause, not its manner; manipulation arguments in the free-will literature already use a
  divine designer. Non-competition accounts and Edwards's "new sense" are examined.
- **A condition on manner (§6).** The traditions' descriptions converge on healing rather than
  installing. The paper states this in interventionist terms: grace targets the evaluative
  capacity, not the values. It is offered as a proposal, with what it leaves open: a norm of
  proper function, and whether divine aim at a capacity can be told from aim at its verdict.
- **Assurance (§7).** Certainty of grace cannot rest on introspection of one's own conversion.
  The main doctrines of assurance place certainty in the object of faith or in a divine
  testimony, not in the self-inspected profile.

## Relation to Paper 1

The constraint is adapted from Paper 1, which argues the same structural point about formal
models of value change. Section 3 restates the argument in a self-contained form, so nothing in
Paper 4 depends on Paper 1's other claims.
