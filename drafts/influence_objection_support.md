# Support notes for the author: "Is Class G just ordinary interpersonal influence?"

**Status (2026-09-26).** The answer is now §8.4 of Paper 1. It chooses answer A in a restricted form, with an *anchored* criterion (endorsable ex ante by the pre-change standards). It was drafted with Claude in the project's chat and revised by the author, not written by the author alone.

**This is working material, not paper text.** The Paper 1 development plan
(`drafts/paper1_restructure_plan.md`, phase 2.2) names this as the most dangerous objection for
a philosophy-of-action audience, and assigns the answer to the author. These notes set out the
objection, the two answers the plan allows, and the hooks each answer has in the current text.
The text must choose one answer explicitly.

## 1. The objection, as a referee would put it

As defined in §8, Class G is:
- external, not generated by the agent's own dynamics (condition 10);
- gated by the agent's receptivity (condition 9);
- mode-selective, contracting destructive directions and leaving agency open (conditions 2–5);
- not tracking, so it commands no path.

Isn't that just a friend, a mentor, a book, an encounter that gives reasons? Callard herself
describes aspiration through mentors and models. If Class G is ordinary influence, the paper's
conclusion becomes "control does not model conversion, but encounter and persuasion do". That
is plausible, but it is not new.

## 2. Answer A: Class G is narrower than ordinary influence

The claim to be made is that one condition excludes ordinary persuasion. The best candidate is
**condition 10 read together with the unanchored restriction of §7.1**.

- **Ordinary persuasion is anchored.** When a friend gives reasons, the agent assesses them by
  the standards she already has. The change she undergoes is computed by her own evaluation of
  the new input. In the paper's terms that is the endogenous horn: the field is external, but the
  reorganization happens inside the agent's own evaluative structure $\langle F_0\rangle$. It is
  also the anchored case §7.1 cedes to Callard.
  - The formal counterpart is already in §7.4. Bradley's *first* model of preference change
    explains change by new information, with fundamental desires held fixed. Persuasion by reasons
    is that model: the input is external and the standard is fixed.
- **What Class G then covers** is influence whose efficacy does *not* run through the agent's
  current standards, but is gated by her receptivity. §7.6's non-vacuity check already names the
  candidates:
  - change undergone rather than undertaken;
  - non-deliberative moral change (Arpaly's Huck Finn);
  - the conversion of the actively hostile (though the hostile case may belong to the U-limit;
    see §9).
- **What the author would have to add.** A criterion for "efficacy runs through current
  standards" that is not question-begging. One option is counterfactual: hold the agent's
  current evaluative standards fixed, and ask whether the input would still produce the change.
  - If it would, because the agent can assess the input as a reason by her present lights, the
    influence is anchored and falls outside Class G.
  - If it would not, the influence is a Class G candidate.
- **Costs.**
  - Callard's reply returns in a new form. For any apparent Class G case she can redescribe some
    prior thread as the proleptic grasp (§7.6 already states this reply).
  - The criterion has to be applicable without first knowing the answer.
- **Hooks in the current text:** §7.1 (the concession to Callard), §7.4 (Bradley's first model),
  §7.6 (the non-vacuity class), §8.2 and §8.3 (conditions 9 and 10), §6.2 (the endogenous horn).

## 3. Answer B: accept the identification

The claim to be made is that Class G *is* the structure of encounter, and the contribution is
showing that only this form survives.

- The trilemma excludes self-generated change, forced change and optimization. What remains has
  the shape of an encounter: external, gated by receptivity, and selective about what it changes.
- On this answer the paper does not claim that Class G is rarer than ordinary influence. It
  claims that the *formal models* of value change (section 7) cannot represent what ordinary
  encounters do, and that the reason is structural: they hold an evaluative point fixed.
- **Costs.**
  - Class G loses independent content, and the conclusion is less surprising. The satisfiability
    and discrimination results of §8.3 still stand, but they then show that the profile of
    encounter is consistent and excludes the near-misses. They no longer show that some narrower
    profile is.
  - The section should say plainly what the formal apparatus adds over "encounter and
    persuasion": presumably the costing of the exogenous horn (§6.3) and the case analysis of
    section 7.
- **Hooks in the current text:** §8 introduction ("the shape of the space the trilemma leaves empty"),
  §8.3 (satisfiability), section 7.

## 4. An assessment, offered as input, not as the decision

- **Answer A** gives Class G content and fits the unanchored restriction the paper already
  accepts. But it needs a new argument, the non-question-begging mediation criterion, and it
  reopens the exchange with Callard.
- **Answer B** is safer and shorter. It makes the paper's novelty rest wholly on section 7 and
  the costing.
- Both are defensible. What does not work is leaving the objection unanswered, or answering it
  with A in one place and B in another.
